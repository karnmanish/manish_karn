Technical papers

M.Tech project

Analysis of Ground Current to Minimize the Potential Induced Degradation (PID) Effect in Solar PV Panels

Potential Induced Degradation (PID) is a long-term reliability concern for grid-connected photovoltaic (PV) systems, in which high system voltage drives a leakage current between the active solar cells and the grounded module frame. This paper analyzes the ground-current pathway responsible for PID and presents a hardware prototype for sensing this current on an operating PV system. The parasitic capacitance formed between a PV panel and earth is examined for a transformerless inverter topology, and a resonant-circuit model is used to relate this capacitance to the observable leakage-current oscillation period. A sensing circuit built around TL082 operational amplifiers, together with a three-phase IGBT-based pulse-width-modulated inverter, was designed, assembled, and tested on a 400 W outdoor PV array. The inverter was characterized across a range of modulation indices, and the sensing prototype was used to measure the common-mode voltage, and hence the ground current, between the module frame and earth during operation. The measured leakage current oscillates within a range consistent with parasitic-capacitance-driven behavior reported in the literature, supporting ground-current monitoring as a low-cost, field-deployable indicator of PID risk in grid-connected PV installations.

B.E. projects

Design and Construction of a Path Detector Using NE555 Timer, LM324N Comparator, and AND Gate Logic

This paper presents the design, implementation, and performance evaluation of a low-cost optical path detector intended for use as the sensing front-end of line-following robotic platforms. The system distinguishes between a black line and a white background using two Light Dependent Resistor (LDR) based optical channels. An NE555 timer operating in astable mode periodically pulses the emitter LEDs, an LM324N quad operational amplifier is configured as a dual-channel voltage comparator to evaluate reflected-light levels against adjustable reference thresholds, and an MC74F08 quad 2-input AND gate combines the two comparator outputs to drive an audible buzzer only when both sensing channels simultaneously indicate an off-path condition. This logical AND combination allows the system to distinguish between partial deviation, in which only a visual LED indicator is activated, and complete path loss, in which both LED and audible alarms are triggered. The circuit was prototyped on a breadboard, verified functionally, and subsequently transferred to a printed circuit board (PCB) for a more robust implementation. Experimental testing showed reliable discrimination between black and white surfaces on both straight and curved paths, with a nominal +5 V logic supply derived from a 9 V battery via a resistive voltage-drop network. The design demonstrates that dependable optical line-sensing can be achieved using discrete analog and digital ICs, without a microcontroller, at very low component cost, making it a suitable educational platform for introducing first-year electrical and electronics engineering students to sensor interfacing, comparator design, and combinational logic.

Design and Construction of a DC Voltage Supplier with Seven-Segment Display

This paper presents the design and construction of a variable DC power supply integrated with a seven-segment digital display. A variable DC power supply is a device that delivers electrical power to a load and allows the user to vary the output voltage within a specified range. Such a unit is among the most frequently used instruments in electronic workshops and laboratories, where a fluctuation-free, adjustable output is required. The proposed circuit is built around an adjustable three-terminal variable-voltage regulator LM338, a fixed 5 V regulator LM7805, and an LM337 negative regulator used for current regulation. The 220 V AC mains supply is stepped down by a transformer to a secondary output of −12 V–0–+12 V AC at 3 A. A full-wave bridge rectifier converts this to pulsating DC, which is smoothed by filter capacitors to eliminate ripple. Both the positive and negative half-cycles are used to obtain positive and negative DC outputs simultaneously. The digital display section employs the CMOS analog-to-digital converter IC ICL7107CPLZ to measure and display the regulated output voltage and current on seven-segment displays.

Design and Implementation of an Arduino-Based Autonomous Line-Following Robot with Onboard Distance Measurement

Line following is one of the most fundamental tasks in mobile robotics and serves as a stepping stone toward more advanced autonomous navigation systems. This paper presents the design, implementation, and evaluation of an autonomous line-following robot capable of tracking a black line drawn on a white surface using an array of optical (IR-LED/photodiode) sensors. The four-sensor array gives the platform precise and flexible path tracking, while two DC gear motors, driven through an L293D H-bridge under Arduino Uno control, provide differential steering. A proportional duty-cycle control scheme adjusts the pulse-width-modulated (PWM) signal supplied to each motor so that the robot can travel straight, turn, or execute sharp turns in response to the sensor pattern, and it re-acquires the line autonomously by sweeping in a circular search pattern whenever it loses track. An auxiliary infrared break-beam sensor coupled to a wheel is used to build a low-cost digital revolution counter, whose count is converted into a travelled-distance estimate and shown on an onboard liquid-crystal display (LCD). The complete circuit was first verified in Proteus simulation and then implemented on hardware. Practical measurements of motor speed against PWM duty cycle, together with a comparison between the constructed tachometer and a commercial reference instrument (error within approximately 6–12%), are reported. The final electromechanical platform measures 192 × 100 × 70 mm³, weighs approximately 200 g, and achieves a minimum turning radius of 100 mm at a velocity of about 24.2 cm/s. The system demonstrates that a low-cost, interdisciplinary combination of optical sensing, embedded control, and differential-drive kinematics can yield a reliable line-tracking platform suitable for automated-guidance applications such as industrial material carriers, museum tour guides, and household or educational robotics.

Performance Monitoring and Illumination Redesign of Academic Buildings With Feasibility Assessment of a Hybrid Solar Power System: A Case Study of Kathmandu University

Appendix
Interior lighting is one of the dominant consumers of electrical energy in institutional buildings, commonly accounting for more than 30% of total electrical consumption. This paper reports a combined energy-audit and lighting-redesign study, together with a feasibility assessment of a hybrid solar power system, carried out at Blocks 2, 8 and 9 of Kathmandu University. Watt-hour load profiles were compiled for each block by on-site survey across working and non-working hours and across summer and winter seasons. On-site illuminance was measured with a digital lux meter and cross-checked against DIALux simulation, revealing that the fluorescent- and CFL-based lighting in Block 8 fell well short of recommended levels, whereas the LED lighting of Block 9 was closer to, though still below, the recommended illuminance. Replacement of the existing fixtures with LED luminaires was evaluated for Blocks 2 and 8, yielding an energy-consumption reduction of approximately 60% and simple payback periods of about 4 years and 12 years, respectively. A hybrid solar photovoltaic system was sized for the combined load of Blocks 8 and 9, requiring approximately 226 modules of 250 W (56.5 kWp), a 16-unit, 200 Ah battery bank, a single 292 A charge controller and a 100 kW hybrid inverter. The existing loop-in wiring scheme of Block 9 was also documented and analyzed for cable and protective-device sizing. The results support a priority order of solar photovoltaic supply, grid supply, battery storage and diesel back-up, and recommend LED retrofit and structured solar deployment as cost-effective measures for reducing the University's energy footprint.

Technical articles

A Field Study of the Sunkoshi Hydropower Station: Generation, Transmission, and Operational Challenges

This paper presents a field study of the Sunkoshi Hydropower Station, a run-of-river plant located in Lamoshangu, Sindhupalchowk district, Nepal, conducted as part of an undergraduate field visit by the Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering, Kathmandu University. The station, commissioned in January 1972 through the cooperation of the Water Conservancy and Electric Power Ministry of the People's Republic of China and the Government of Nepal, has an installed capacity of 10.05 MW across three Francis turbine-generator units. The study documents the complete generation chain of the plant, including the intake and head works, the open canal and penstock conveyance system, the powerhouse and its electromechanical equipment, the electromechanical governing and control system, and the switchyard, protection, and transmission arrangement that delivers power at 66 kV toward Kathmandu and at 11 kV to surrounding districts. Particular attention is given to the operational challenges faced by the plant, including seasonal discharge variation, sedimentation and solid-waste intrusion at the intake, and the structural damage caused by landslides and the 2015 earthquake, which together reduced the station's output to approximately 60% of its rated capacity for an extended period. The study concludes by relating the observed engineering practices to the broader context of hydropower development, local electrification, and irrigation in Nepal, and highlights the value of field-based learning in bridging classroom theory with practical power system operation.

Fabrication and Training on Solar-Powered Mobile Phone Charger with Torch: A Community Education Project in Laduk VDC, Nepal

This paper presents the design, fabrication, and community-based training of a low-cost solar-powered mobile phone charger integrated with an LED torch, carried out under the Community Education Project (CEP) of the Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering, Kathmandu University. The activity was conducted in Laduk Village Development Committee (VDC), Dolakha district, Nepal, an off-grid mountainous area with limited access to electricity and technical education. A team of five undergraduate students, under faculty supervision, organized a four-day outreach program that combined household-level need assessment with hands-on technical training for secondary-level students at two local schools. Students were introduced to basic principles of electricity and safe handling practices before being trained to assemble a solar-powered charger unit using locally available and economical components. The paper discusses the motivation, planning, fabrication approach, and pedagogical methodology used to deliver the training, along with the outcomes and broader implications of the project for rural electrification and technical skill development. The project demonstrates that small-scale, student-led interventions combining device fabrication with hands-on training can help bridge the technical education gap in remote communities.
